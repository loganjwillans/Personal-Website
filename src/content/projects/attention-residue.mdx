A decision log tells you what a team chose. It does not tell you what the team almost chose, or
what it chose not to talk about. I started keeping a second log to see whether that discarded
material had a shape.

<Card title="Method" label="The second log" color="blue">

For eight design reviews I wrote down every idea that surfaced and did not survive, with one line
about why the conversation moved on. I did not record who said what. That made it easier to look at
the ideas rather than at the room.

The reviews were ordinary: a mix of interface decisions, scope arguments and last-minute concerns.
Nothing about them was special, which was the point.

</Card>

The most common reason an idea disappeared was not disagreement. It was timing.

<LooseCard title="The pattern underneath" color="cream">

Sequencing proposals dominated the residue. People could agree that a change should happen and
still disagree about when, and the "when" conversation almost always ran out of time. The ideas
that survived were the ones that could be decided in a sentence.

</LooseCard>

<Callout label="Caveat" title="A diary, not a study" tone="blue" foot="Enough to notice something, not enough to generalise.">

Eight reviews is a small, self-selected sample. The useful output is the shape of the list, not the
counts on it.

</Callout>

<Quote>

We didn't decide against it. We just ran out of meeting.

</Quote>

The practical change is modest: I leave the last five minutes of a review for the residue. It is
far cheaper to write down an idea at the moment it is dropped than to reconstruct it later.
