A colleague kept a spreadsheet of clause patterns she had seen before. It worked well, but only for
her, and it grew slower every month. The toolkit is that spreadsheet turned into one command: it
reads a document, flags the patterns and prints a short checklist.

<Card title="Design constraints" label="Scope" color="blue">

- It runs offline. The documents are confidential and should never leave the machine.
- It prints a checklist, not a score. A score invites argument; a checklist invites action.
- It stays boring. Nothing in the output should surprise the person reading it.

</Card>

The first version tried to classify clauses by type. That was clever and wrong. Reviewers did not
want a taxonomy — they wanted to know what to check next. The current version prints three columns:
what it found, where, and what to confirm.

<CodeBlock
  title="terminal"
  language="bash"
  code={`$ toolkit check agreement.pdf

11 patterns loaded
  42  indemnity cap            check against playbook §4
 118  auto-renewal             confirm notice period
 203  governing law            confirm jurisdiction

3 items to review · 2s`}
/>

<LooseCard title="The deliberate limit" color="cream">

The toolkit does not learn. Every pattern is added by hand, which is slower but makes the output
predictable. For a review aid, predictability is worth more than cleverness — somebody has to trust
the checklist enough to use it under time pressure.

</LooseCard>

<Callout label="Next" title="Where it goes from here" tone="green" foot="Only if the checklist earns its place.">

The interesting question is not which patterns to add next. It is whether reviewers change what
they check when the list is in front of them. That needs observation, not another feature.

</Callout>

<Quote>

Design the output for the moment someone is tired, not the moment they are curious.

</Quote>
