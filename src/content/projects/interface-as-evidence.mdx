Specifications describe what a team intended. Interfaces describe what it shipped. When the two
disagree, the interface is usually the more reliable document, because it had to survive contact
with real use.

<Card title="Three records of a decision" label="The argument" color="green">

1. **The spec** — what we said we would build. Easy to write, and easy to leave behind.
2. **The artefact** — what exists now, including the compromises made under deadline.
3. **The trace** — how people actually used it, which is the only record that can tell us whether
   the first two mattered.

Most teams keep the first record carefully and lose the other two. Then they wonder why the design
system no longer matches the product.

</Card>

A component library is the easiest place to see the gap. The library is the artefact; usage data is
the trace. If a pattern has no usages, the system has quietly changed without anyone writing that
change down.

<LooseCard title="A small exercise" color="blush">

Pick one component from a library you know. Find every place it is used, then find the note that
introduced it. Compare the two. The distance between them is the real design history — usually a
story about deadlines, or one team that needed something slightly different.

</LooseCard>

<Quote attribution="From a review of our own library">

The spec said the component was for errors. Every real use was for empty states. We had documented
the intention and missed the function.

</Quote>

<Callout label="Practical version" title="Keep the artefact next to the decision" tone="green" foot="A disagreement between the two is usually the next decision hiding in plain sight.">

When you write down a decision, link to the component that carries it. If the component changes
and the note does not, that is worth investigating rather than tidying up.

</Callout>

My proposal is simple: treat the working interface as evidence, not as the outcome of a process
that ended at the spec. The record survives because somebody had to maintain the thing.
