Contract review is the least glamorous part of contract management, and the part where the money is. A reviewer opens a redlined document, compares it against a playbook, checks a clause library, and decides whether each change is acceptable. In our product, that meant four panes, three scrollbars, and a spreadsheet someone kept on a second monitor.

This is the story of rebuilding that surface — and of the two decisions that did most of the work.

## Context

IntelAgree's reviewer workspace had grown feature by feature for years. Every request added a panel, because adding a panel was the cheapest way to say yes. By the time I joined the squad, the default view had eleven interactive regions and a legal reviewer's first action was usually to close four of them.

The team knew it was heavy. What we didn't know was which parts were load-bearing.

## The problem

We framed it as a single question: **can a reviewer answer "what changed and does it matter?" without leaving the document?**

Everything else was secondary. Not "make it prettier", not "reduce clicks" — those are outcomes, not problems. The measurable version was time-to-first-decision per redline, and the qualitative version was how confident reviewers felt when they accepted a change.

## What we heard

We sat with six reviewers across three customers, watching them work through live contracts rather than test data. Three findings shaped everything after:

- **The document is the source of truth, not the panel.** Every reviewer scrolled the document first, then looked for corroboration. Our layout did the opposite — it put the metadata panel first in reading order.
- **Reviewers trust their own marks.** They annotated with highlighters and comments even when the system had already flagged a clause. The annotation wasn't redundant; it was how they remembered their reasoning.
- **The playbook is a memory aid, not a checklist.** Reviewers rarely read it top to bottom. They jumped to the clause they were already worried about.

## The decisions that mattered

<Decision
  title="Put the clause context next to the change, not in a side panel"
  tradeoff="The document column got narrower, and we lost the ability to pin more than one clause at a time."
>
  We moved deviation flags and playbook guidance inline, anchored to the change itself. The side
  panel stayed for navigation and search, but it stopped being the place where judgement happened.
  Reviewers no longer had to hold a clause reference in their head while reading a paragraph.
  
  This is the change people noticed in the first five minutes of testing, and the one that survived
  every later round of cuts.
</Decision>

<Decision
  title="Treat reviewer annotations as first-class data"
  tradeoff="We had to store and sync private notes per reviewer, which added a permissions surface we'd previously avoided."
>
  Instead of trying to replace the highlighter habit, we absorbed it. Reviewers can mark any change
  with a private note, visible only to them, that persists across sessions. Their reasoning stays
  attached to the thing they were reasoning about.
  
  It also gave us something unexpected: a signal about which clauses consistently needed
  explanation, which fed back into the playbook.
</Decision>

## What shipped

The final surface has one primary column and one utility column. Everything that used to live in a panel is either inline, in the utility column, or gone. We deleted more than we added — nine panels became two, and four of them had no usage data worth defending.

The confidence number is the one I care about. Speed can be bought by hiding things. Confidence is what you get when the surface matches how the work is actually done.

## What I'd do differently

We shipped the inline annotations in the same release as the layout change, which made it impossible to attribute the confidence gain to either one. In hindsight, annotations should have been a second release with their own measurement window. We were lucky the story held up; we couldn't prove it.
