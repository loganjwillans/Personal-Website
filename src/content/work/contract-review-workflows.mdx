Contract review is the least glamorous part of contract management, and the part where the money
is. A reviewer opens a redlined document, compares it against a playbook, checks a clause library,
and decides whether each change is acceptable. In our product that meant four panes, three
scrollbars, and a spreadsheet someone kept on a second monitor.

<Card title="The question we framed" label="Context" color="blue">

IntelAgree's reviewer workspace had grown feature by feature for years. Every request added a panel,
because adding a panel was the cheapest way to say yes. By the time I joined the squad there were
eleven interactive regions, and a reviewer's first action was usually to close four of them.

We framed the problem as one question: **can a reviewer answer "what changed and does it matter?"
without leaving the document?** Not "make it prettier" and not "reduce clicks" — those are
outcomes, not problems.

</Card>

## What we heard

We sat with six reviewers across three customers, watching them work through live contracts rather
than test data.

- **The document is the source of truth, not the panel.** Reviewers scrolled the document first,
  then looked for corroboration. Our layout put the metadata panel first in reading order.
- **Reviewers trust their own marks.** They annotated with highlighters even when the system had
  already flagged a clause. The annotation was how they remembered their reasoning.
- **The playbook is a memory aid, not a checklist.** Reviewers jumped to the clause they were
  already worried about rather than reading it top to bottom.

## The decisions that mattered

<Callout
  label="Decision"
  title="Put clause context next to the change, not in a side panel"
  color="green"
  foot="Trade-off: the document column got narrower, and we lost the ability to pin more than one clause at a time."
>
  We moved deviation flags and playbook guidance inline, anchored to the change itself. The side
  panel stayed for navigation and search, but it stopped being where judgement happened. This was
  the change people noticed in the first five minutes of testing, and the one that survived every
  later round of cuts.
</Callout>

<Callout
  label="Decision"
  title="Treat reviewer annotations as first-class data"
  color="green"
  foot="Trade-off: we had to store and sync private notes per reviewer, adding a permissions surface we had avoided."
>
  Instead of replacing the highlighter habit, we absorbed it. Reviewers can mark any change with a
  private note that persists across sessions, so their reasoning stays attached to the thing they
  were reasoning about. It also gave us a signal about which clauses consistently needed
  explanation, which fed back into the playbook.
</Callout>

The confidence number is the one I care about. Speed can be bought by hiding things. Confidence is
what you get when the surface matches how the work is actually done.

<Quote attribution="A reviewer, after the session">

I stopped keeping the spreadsheet on the second monitor after the first week. That was when I knew
the new layout had worked.

</Quote>

<Callout label="What I would change" title="Separate the releases" tone="blue" foot="We shipped annotations and layout together, so we could not attribute the gain.">

The annotations should have been a second release with their own measurement window. We were lucky
the story held up; we could not prove it.

</Callout>
